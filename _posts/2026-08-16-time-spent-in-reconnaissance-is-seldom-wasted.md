title: "Time Spent in Reconnaissance Is Seldom Wasted"
---

One of the classic and most basic military ideas is: know thy enemy.
Before conducting any kind of operation, you want to understand as much of the environment as you reasonably can. The ground. The weather. The opposition. The likely routes. What could change. What you don't know.

Time spent in reconnaissance is seldom wasted.

That doesn’t mean endlessly gathering information or trying to build the perfect plan. Eventually, you have to act. But the better your understanding of the environment, the better the decisions available to you when reality inevitably diverges from the plan.

The same principle applies remarkably well to risk.

Organisations are often very good at documenting what they already know. There are risk registers, controls, governance forums, incident processes and policies.
The more interesting question is whether those things describe reality.

That requires reconnaissance.

Spend time understanding the technology. Talk to the people operating it. Look at incidents. Understand how the architecture has changed. Ask where teams are taking manual actions. Find the dependencies nobody talks about because they've always been there.

And then ask the uncomfortable question:
Why?
Why does this control exist?
Why is this risk rated this way?
Why do we believe this mitigation actually reduces the risk?
Why has this process remained unchanged while the technology around it has completely transformed?
Why does this incident keep happening?
Why are we accepting this risk?

"Why" is uncomfortable because it exposes assumptions.
Sometimes the answer is perfectly reasonable. Sometimes nobody really knows. The process exists because the process has always existed. The risk rating was inherited. The control sounds reassuring but nobody has considered whether it still operates as intended.

That discomfort is useful.
One of the easiest traps in risk management is confusing the documented environment with the real environment.
They are rarely identical.

The point of good risk management isn't to create increasingly sophisticated descriptions of risk. It's to understand the environment well enough to make better decisions about it.
That means looking beyond the register.
It means understanding what is changing, where complexity is increasing, what incidents are trying to tell you and where your confidence may exceed your evidence.
And occasionally it means being the irritating person in the room who keeps asking:
Why?

Good reconnaissance won't predict everything.
But when something unexpected happens, you'll understand the terrain considerably better than if you'd simply trusted the map.
